Disney Parks Industrial Engineering Case Study

A case study from Walt Disney Parks & Resorts' industrial engineering team (Fall 2026), done with Aarnav Bhat and Justin Llamera. The festival runs for four fall weekends and has 120 booth spaces for food, merchandise and activities. The case asked three questions: How many guests will come? How many booths of each type are needed? How should the festival grow? We forecast about 68,000 visits, picked a booth mix that keeps lines short, and recommended staying open 12 hours a day instead of 10.

68,000visits forecast for 2026 (+21%)
45 / 21 / 27food, merchandise and activity booths
~6 minlongest wait on the busiest day
$55Kyearly gain from 12-hour days

Excel models

Excel forecast model. Part 1 is built in four linked steps. The model is modular, so any input can be changed and the forecast updates.

2026 forecast from each trend method (history only)

There are only five years of attendance data (2021–2025), which is too little for complex models like ARIMA. We used five simple trend methods and took their average. The five forecasts are within about 1,650 visits of each other.

Linear trend and ensemble (Excel)

=INTERCEPT(Attendance, t) + SLOPE(Attendance, t) * 6
=SUMPRODUCT(Forecasts, Include) / SUM(Include)

Hold-out error: forecasting 2024 and 2025 from earlier years

To test the methods, we fit each one on 2021–2023 to forecast 2024, then on 2021–2024 to forecast 2025, and measured the error. Every method forecast too low, so we added the 2026 drivers in the next step.

Error metric

APE  = ABS(Forecast - Actual) / Actual
MAPE = AVERAGE(APE_2024, APE_2025)

From the trend baseline to the 68,000-visit forecast

We added four changes planned for 2026 that the past data doesn't include. Marketing and the anniversary are percentage increases on the baseline. New markets and the concert are added as extra visits.

  • Marketing: +20% spend × 0.12 advertising elasticity (meta-analysis of 751 studies)
  • New markets: 700,000 new residents × local attendance rate × 8% distance-decay factor
  • Anniversary + concert: our own estimates (3% increase, 1,500 concert-only visits)

Forecast build-up

= Baseline * (1 + Spend * Elasticity) * (1 + Halo)
  + ReachVisits + ConcertVisits

1,000 simulated 2026 attendance totals

Each of the five uncertain inputs has a low, expected and high value. The model draws each one from a triangular distribution and recalculates the forecast 1,000 times. The middle 80% of results falls between 66,359 and 70,257 visits.

66,359P10
68,312P50 (median)
70,257P90

Triangular sampling with RAND() (Excel)

=IF(U < (Mode-Min)/(Max-Min),
    Min + SQRT(U*(Max-Min)*(Mode-Min)),
    Max - SQRT((1-U)*(Max-Min)*(Max-Mode)))

98.9% of trials exceed 65,000 visits. Standard deviation: 1,489.

Excel booth mix model. Part 2 decides how to split the 120 booth spaces between food, merchandise and activities. It uses the Part 1 peak day (10,808 guests) and is built in four steps.

Booths needed to handle the lunch peak vs. the final mix

Minimum from queueingRecommended mix

For each category, I turned the peak day into transactions per hour at the busiest hour, then used Little's Law to find the fewest booths that keep each booth under 85% busy. Activities booths take two spaces each.

10,808guests on the peak day
101of 120 spaces needed at minimum
19spaces left to allocate

Minimum booths (Excel)

Arrivals = Guests * Part * Txns * PeakShare
Booths   = CEILING(Arrivals / (Rate * 0.85), 1)

Peak-hour wait at each booth type with the recommended mix

Each booth is treated as a single-server queue (M/M/1). The wait grows quickly as a booth gets close to 100% busy, which is why the model caps peak use at 85%. Guests start leaving the line after about 5 minutes, and all of them leave by 20 minutes.

Wait in line, M/M/1 (Excel)

Util = Arrivals / (Booths * Rate)
Wait = Util / (Rate * (1 - Util)) * 60

Daily vendor profit for the recommended mix vs. simple rules

The optimizer sheet lists every mix that fills the 120 spaces, 943 in total, and checks each one against the wait limit and the variety rules. 49 mixes pass, and the model picks the one with the highest vendor profit.

  • No booth type over 85% busy at the peak hour
  • Food is at most half the space; merchandise is at least 15% of booths; activities take 20% to 45% of the space
  • More activity booths keep guests longer, which adds food sales

Pick the best feasible mix (Excel)

Score = IF(Feasible, Contribution, -1)
Best  = INDEX(Mix, MATCH(MAX(Score), Score, 0))

Food booth utilization at the lunch peak under each Part 1 scenario

I reran the recommended mix on the low and high peak days from Part 1. It holds on low and expected days. On a high-attendance Saturday, food booths go over 100% busy and the lines stop clearing.

That is a capacity problem that a different mix can't fix, so it leads into Part 3: adding hours or days to spread the crowd out.

~6 minlongest expected wait
102%food load on a high day

Excel growth options model. Part 3 compares three ways to grow the festival: longer days (10 to 12 hours), adding Fridays, or adding a fifth weekend. Each option is tested under low, expected and high demand.

Added yearly revenue and cost for each option (expected demand)

Added revenueAdded cost

New visits come from people who couldn't come before, minus visits that just move from another day. Revenue per visit is admission plus a 15% share of vendor sales, minus variable cost. Costs are built per operating day and per added hour.

$55K12-hour days, year-1 gain
$36KFridays, year-1 gain
$3K5th weekend, year-1 gain

Net-new visits and break-even (Excel)

NetNew    = GrossNew - Shifted
BreakEven = AddedCost / NetRevenuePerVisit

5-year NPV: low, expected and high demand

ExpectedLow and high

Each option's yearly cash flow is discounted over 5 years at 8%, with revenue growing 4% a year and cost 3%. Longer days are the only option that still makes money if demand comes in low. Fridays have the highest upside but lose money in the low case.

NPV (Excel)

= -OneTimeCost + NPV(8%, Year1:Year5)

Food booth utilization at the lunch peak on a high-attendance day

All three options bring the high-day food peak back under 100%. Longer days do it by spreading the lunch rush over a longer day (peak share drops from 15% to 13%). Fridays and a fifth weekend depend on guests choosing to move their visit.

I also checked vendor economics: a food booth would lose about $38 per Friday on expected demand, which makes Fridays hard to staff.

Weighted decision score (out of 5)

Each option is scored 1 to 5 on financial return (30%), peak relief (25%), risk and reversibility (20%), vendor and staff feasibility (15%) and growth headroom (10%). I reran the scores under four other sets of weights, and 12-hour days came out on top every time.

Recommendation: extend to 12-hour days now, keep a fifth weekend as the next step once evenings are proven, and skip full-day Fridays.

Weighted score (Excel)

= SUMPRODUCT(Weights, Scores)

Accounts Payable Invoice Automation

Before a company pays a supplier, its accounts payable (AP) team checks the invoice. Is there a purchase order? Does the amount match? Was it already paid? I built this process in four parts for 5,000 synthetic invoices from 140 vendors. Part 1 sets up the checks in Excel. Part 2 uses Python to predict which invoices need a manual review and how many reviewers the team needs. Part 3 is a Tableau dashboard for managers. Part 4 uses Claude, an AI model, to read the invoice PDFs, so clean invoices get approved without anyone typing them in.

5,000invoices ($90.7M) run through 8 checks
2reviewers finish 96% of reviews within 8 business hours
70%of invoices approved with no one touching them
97.8%of invoice PDFs read correctly by Claude, for $3.98 total

How it was built

Excel control workbook. Each invoice is linked to its vendor, purchase order (PO) and exchange rate with XLOOKUP. It then goes through the same checks an AP team runs before paying.

Invoice value held up by each check (USD millions)

The checks are: missing or invalid PO, invoice amount over the PO, currency different from the PO, payment terms different from the vendor's, inactive vendor, and duplicate invoice number. The rules flagged 1,514 invoices, and they match the real review decisions 96.9% of the time.

1,514invoices flagged (30%)
96.9%match actual reviews
$18.5Mover the PO amount

PO and duplicate checks (Excel)

=IF(PO="","Missing PO",
   IF(COUNTIFS(PO_List,PO)=0,"Invalid PO","Matched"))
=IF(COUNTIFS(Vendor,V,InvNo,N)>1,"Duplicate","Unique")

Invoices by priority level

Each failed check adds points. A duplicate, missing PO, amount over the PO or inactive vendor adds 3. A currency mismatch or high-risk vendor adds 2. A terms mismatch adds 1. Large invoices get 1 or 2 extra points. Six points or more is high priority. The points live on an Assumptions sheet, so a manager can change them.

Each invoice also gets a recommended action, like "hold payment and investigate duplicate".

155high-priority invoices
$10.0Mvalue of high priority
9action codes

Priority level (Excel)

=IF(Score>=6,"High",IF(Score>=3,"Medium","Low"))

When the 4,157 payments went out

The workbook also tracks what happens after the checks. 22% of payments went out after the due date, worth $17.7M. In the review log, 78% of reviews finished within 24 hours. The average review took 18.4 hours from entering the queue to being done.

Python model and staffing simulation. Part 2 trains a model to predict which invoices need a manual review. It then simulates the review queue to find how many reviewers the team needs. Built with pandas and scikit-learn in Google Colab.

Test results on the last 1,000 invoices

Precision (flagged invoices that needed review)Recall (reviews it caught)

I trained on the first 4,000 invoices by date and tested on the last 1,000 (Nov 19, 2025 to Jan 5, 2026). That way the model never learns from the future. I compared a baseline that never flags anything (75.7% accurate), logistic regression and a random forest. The random forest was more precise, so I picked it. I set its cutoff so it keeps catching at least 85% of reviews.

97.3%accuracy
92.2%precision
97.1%recall

Most important inputs to the random forest

The top five inputs are all Excel checks from Part 1. In this data, an invoice needs a review exactly when one of those checks fails, so the model mostly learns the checks again. That's why its accuracy is so high.

The useful part is the risk score. It ranks which invoice a reviewer should open first, and it's used that way in the staffing simulation and in Part 4.

Reviews finished within 8 business hours

First in, first outHighest risk first

The simulation replays all 1,499 real reviews, using their arrival times and review lengths from the log, through a queue with 1 to 4 reviewers working 9 to 5 on weekdays. It compares first-in-first-out with opening the highest-risk invoice first.

Result: 2 reviewers with highest-risk-first finish 96% of reviews within 8 business hours. High-risk invoices wait about 13 minutes on average, down from 1.8 hours.

AI invoice reading with the Claude API. Parts 1–3 assume someone already typed every invoice into the system. Part 4 automates that step. I made 836 realistic invoice PDFs from the test-period data, including 128 scans. Then I compared two ways of reading them: hand-written rules with OCR, and Claude.

What happened to the 1,000 test invoices (Claude)

  1. Claude reads each PDF and returns the vendor, invoice number, dates, PO, currency and totals as JSON.
  2. The invoice is matched to its vendor by tax ID and to its PO. It then goes through the Part 1 checks.
  3. Data checks look for mistakes. Do subtotal and tax add up to the total? Is the due date the invoice date plus the terms? If not, a person fixes it first.
  4. Clean invoices are approved automatically. The rest go to the review queue from Part 2, ranked by risk.

Claude call (Python)

client.messages.create(model="claude-haiku-4-5",
  tools=[record_invoice], tool_choice=forced,
  messages=[{"role": "user", "content": [pdf, prompt]}])

Invoice PDFs with every field read correctly

Rules + OCRClaude

The invoices use five supplier layouts, with different date formats (11/18/2025, 17.11.2025, 18th November 2025) and number formats (1.234,56 €). I wrote the rules for four of the layouts. The fifth stood in for a new supplier the rules had never seen.

The rules were perfect on clean digital PDFs they were written for, 80% on scans, and 0% on the new layout. Claude stayed at 94–99% on everything with no templates.

Claude's 18 mistakes out of 836 PDFs

12 of the 18 mistakes were caught by the data checks and sent to a person, so no invoice was approved with wrong data. Most were dates like 07/12/2025 (7 December) read month first. 4 left the due date blank, which is harmless because it can be filled in from the invoice date and terms.

2 scans had one wrong digit in the invoice number. No check catches that, and it could let a duplicate slip through. A real system should compare invoice numbers against each vendor's usual pattern.

Average days from receiving an invoice to approving it

Over the 6.6 weeks of test invoices, Claude brought the share approved with no one touching them from 0% to 70.4%. The most it could reach with perfect reading is 71.2%. Clean invoices are approved the day they arrive, instead of waiting 1–5 days for a person.

All of the 243 invoices with real exceptions were stopped except 1. That one comes from how duplicates are labeled in the source data, not from a reading error.

Loan Application Outcome Forecast (Absorbing Markov Chain)

Models how bank loan applications move through 7 review steps until they are approved, denied or cancelled. It uses 154,152 status changes from the public BPI Challenge 2017 dataset. I trained it on January–September 2016 data and tested it on applications from October–December.

154Kstatus changes used to train the model
3,167expected approvals from 5,049 open applications ($59.8M)
within 6%accuracy for applications already in review
63% vs. 46%predicted vs. actual approval rate overall
Markov chainsProbabilityBacktestingExcel
Details & files
  • Built the transition matrix from every status change and split it into moves between open steps and moves to final outcomes. Solving the fundamental matrix gives each application's chance of being approved, denied or cancelled, and the expected number of steps to finish. Checks confirm every row of probabilities adds up to 1.
  • Backtested on 4,951 applications that were still open 14 days after they were created. For applications already in validation, the predictions were within about 6%. Overall, actual approvals were 17 points lower than the forecast.
  • Most of the gap came from applications at the "Complete" step. The model predicted 1,825 approvals there, and 975 happened. A Markov chain doesn't account for how long an application has been waiting, so the next step would be to recalibrate on recent data and include time spent at each step.
